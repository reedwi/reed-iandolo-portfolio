**The short version:** most owners don't lose their week to one big problem. They lose it to dozens of small, repeating tasks. The fix is to list them, put a rough value on each in hours saved, and solve them one at a time, starting with the ones that pay back fastest. Done systematically, it gives you real time back.

## Working in the business instead of on it

You started your business to do the work you're good at. Somewhere along the way, the business started running you.

Invoices. Scheduling. Follow-ups. Chasing information across five different tools. Copying the same details from one place to another. None of it is hard on its own. Together, it's your whole week, and the work you're actually good at gets squeezed into whatever time is left.

That's the difference between working in the business and working on it.

## Why these tasks survive

Small tasks stick around because each one is easy to justify. It only takes ten minutes. It's not worth fixing. You'll deal with it later.

But ten minutes a day is almost an hour a week, and you don't have one of these tasks. You have dozens. They're also the tasks most likely to slip, because they depend on someone remembering to do them.

## A simple way to find them

This is the approach I use with owners. It doesn't take long.

**1. List everything for a normal week.** Go through a typical week and write down every task that takes time but doesn't need you specifically. Be honest about the small ones. They're the point.

**2. Put a rough value on each.** How long does it take, and how often? Hours saved per month is enough. You don't need precision.

**3. Rank the list together.** Sort by what pays back fastest. A daily ten-minute task often beats a monthly two-hour one. This list becomes the plan, and you can reorder it any time.

**4. Work it from the top.** Solve one at a time, for good. Then keep adding to the list as the business changes.

## What solving them looks like

The fixes are usually small and specific:

- **Inquiries** land in one place and get a reply, even on the days you're heads down.
- **Quotes, invoices, and reminders** go out without you assembling them by hand.
- **Scheduling, onboarding, and handoffs** stop taking a dozen emails.
- **Your numbers** show up in a simple weekly view instead of being pulled together yourself.

Wherever possible, the fix uses the tools you already have. When it doesn't, a small purpose-built tool can fill the gap. I wrote more about that in [Your small business can have its own database](/notes/small-business-own-database).

## Business sense matters as much as the tech

Plenty of people can automate a task. The harder part is knowing which tasks are worth it, and building the fixes so they hold up. That takes both: understanding how the business actually runs, and knowing the technology well enough to solve it properly.

Technology applied to the business this way used to be a big-company advantage. It isn't anymore. It's a competitive advantage in every industry, and it's within reach for small teams now.

## If you only do one thing

This week, keep a running note of every task you do that someone or something else could handle. By Friday, you'll have your list.
